Chapter 1: Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

Q1 Calculating Moles from Mass ($180\text{ g}$ of $H_2O$)

The Core Basics: Atoms and molecules are unimaginably small. Chemists needed a bridge between macroscopic weights we can measure in a lab (grams) and the actual count of atoms/molecules. That bridge is the Mole.

One mole is defined exactly as $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ elementary entities (Avogadro's Number, $N_A$). The molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance expressed in grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$).

Moles (n) = Given Mass (W) / Molar Mass (M)
Step-by-Step Walkthrough:
1. Identify the formula: Water is $H_2O$.
2. Calculate molar mass: Hydrogen has an atomic mass of $1 \text{ g/mol}$ (2 atoms = $2 \times 1 = 2$). Oxygen has an atomic mass of $16 \text{ g/mol}$ (1 atom = $16$).
3. Total Molar Mass of $H_2O = 2 + 16 = 18 \text{ g/mol}$.
4. Apply formula: $n = \frac{180 \text{ g}}{18 \text{ g/mol}} = 10 \text{ moles}$.
Q2 Temperature Effect on Molarity vs. Molality

The Core Basics: Concentration terms tell us how much solute is dissolved in a solvent. But not all concentration units behave the same way when subjected to heating.

• Molarity ($M$): Number of moles of solute dissolved in $1 \text{ litre of solution}$ ($M = \frac{n}{V_{sol} \text{ in L}}$).
• Molality ($m$): Number of moles of solute dissolved in $1 \text{ kg of solvent}$ ($m = \frac{n}{W_{solvent} \text{ in kg}}$).

Why does Molarity change with temperature while Molality is constant?
When you heat a solution, liquids expand (volume $V$ increases). Because volume is in the denominator of Molarity, an increase in volume causes Molarity to decrease. However, mass ($W$) is completely unaffected by temperature. Since Molality relies strictly on masses of solvent, its value remains invariant.
Q3 Fundamental Laws of Chemical Combination

The Core Basics: Chemistry operates under invariant physical laws during reactions. You must know these three foundational laws:

1. Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier): Mass can neither be created nor destroyed during chemical transformations. In any closed system, Total Mass of Reactants = Total Mass of Products.

2. Law of Definite Proportions (Proust): A given chemical compound always contains exactly the same elements combined in the exact same fixed proportions by mass, regardless of where the compound came from or how it was synthesized.

3. Law of Multiple Proportions (Dalton): If two elements can combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in ratios of small whole numbers.

Q4 Empirical & Molecular Formula Numericals

The Core Basics: • Empirical Formula: Expresses the simplest whole-number atom ratio of elements in a compound.
• Molecular Formula: Expresses the exact actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule.

Molecular Formula = n × Empirical Formula

Where the integer multiplier $n = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}}$. To find the empirical formula from percentage composition: divide each element's percentage by its atomic mass to get moles, divide those moles by the smallest mole value to get the simplest ratio, and convert to whole numbers.

Chapter 2: Structure of Atom

Q5 Stability of Copper ($Cu$) and Chromium ($Cr$) Configurations

The Core Basics: According to the Aufbau principle, electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy. However, atoms like Chromium ($Z=24$) and Copper ($Z=29$) violate the expected $3d^4 4s^2$ and $3d^9 4s^2$ patterns, adopting $3d^5 4s^1$ and $3d^{10} 4s^1$ instead.

Why are half-filled ($d^5$) and fully-filled ($d^{10}$) orbitals exceptionally stable?
1. Symmetrical Distribution: Electrons in half-filled and fully-filled subshells enjoy symmetrical charge distribution around the nucleus, minimizing shielding imbalance and electrostatic repulsion.
2. Exchange Energy: Electrons with parallel spins in degenerate orbitals exchange positions and release energy (exchange energy). The greater the number of exchangeable electrons (maximum in $d^5$ and $d^{10}$), the greater the energy release, lowering the overall energy of the atom and maximizing stability.
Q6 Dual Nature of Matter & Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

The Core Basics: • De Broglie Hypothesis: Louis de Broglie proposed that microscopic moving matter (like electrons) exhibits both particle and wave characteristics. The wavelength $\lambda$ associated with a particle of mass $m$ moving at velocity $v$ is given by:

λ = h / p = h / (mv)

• Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: It is fundamentally impossible to simultaneously determine both the exact position ($\Delta x$) and exact momentum ($\Delta p$) of a fast-moving subatomic particle with absolute precision:

Δx · Δp ≥ h / (4π)
Q7 Calculating Orbital Nodes ($3p$ Orbital Example)

The Core Basics: A node is a region in space around the nucleus where the probability density of finding an electron drops precisely to zero.

• Angular Nodes = Azimuthal quantum number (l)
• Radial Nodes = n - l - 1
• Total Nodes = (Angular) + (Radial) = n - 1
Worked Walkthrough for $3p$ Orbital:
1. Principal quantum number $n = 3$.
2. For a $p$ subshell, Azimuthal quantum number $l = 1$.
3. Angular nodes = $l = 1$.
4. Radial nodes = $n - l - 1 = 3 - 1 - 1 = 1$.
5. Total nodes = $n - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2$ (which matches $1 \text{ angular} + 1 \text{ radial}$).
Q8 Pauli's Exclusion Principle

The Core Basics: Pauli's Exclusion Principle states that no two electrons in the same atom can have identical values for all four quantum numbers ($n, l, m_l, m_s$). Since an orbital shares $n, l,$ and $m_l$, the maximum number of electrons an orbital can accommodate is two, and they must have opposite (antiparallel) spins ($+\frac{1}{2}$ and $-\frac{1}{2}$).

Chapter 3: Classification of Elements and Periodicity

Q9 Second Ionization Enthalpy of Sodium ($Na$) vs. Magnesium ($Mg$)

The Core Basics: Ionization Enthalpy ($IE$) is the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom.

Detailed Explanation:
• Sodium ($Na$): Electronic configuration is $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$. Its first ionization enthalpy removes the single $3s$ electron easily. Once gone, the remaining ion ($Na^+$) has the stable, inert noble gas configuration of Neon ($2,8$). Removing a second electron requires breaking into this inner noble gas shell, which demands immense energy.
• Magnesium ($Mg$): Configuration is $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2$. It has two valence electrons, so its second ionization enthalpy is much lower than sodium's because it results in a stable octet.
Q10 Isoelectronic Species & Ionic Radius ($N^{3-}, O^{2-}, F^{-}, Na^{+}, Mg^{2+}$)

The Core Basics: Atoms or ions that contain the exact same number of electrons are called isoelectronic species (e.g., all ions listed have 10 electrons).

Periodic Trend rule:
• Anions ($N^{3-}, O^{2-}, F^{-}$): As negative charge increases, electrons outnumber protons more heavily, increasing inter-electronic repulsion and expanding ionic radius ($N^{3-}$ is largest).
• Cations ($Na^{+}, Mg^{2+}$): As positive charge increases, nuclear pull acts on fewer electrons, shrinking the radius ($Mg^{2+}$ is smallest).
Q11 Noble Gases & Positive Electron Gain Enthalpy

The Core Basics: Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$) is the energy released when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom. Noble gases ($He, Ne, Ar$) possess fully-filled stable octet configurations ($ns^2 np^6$). Forcing an extra electron into them requires energy input (endothermic, hence positive value) because there are no stable vacant orbitals available.

Q12 IUPAC Nomenclature for Superheavy Elements (Atomic Number 114)

The Core Basics: IUPAC naming for elements with $Z > 100$ uses numerical roots: $0 = \text{nil}, 1 = \text{un}, 2 = \text{bi}, 3 = \text{tri}, 4 = \text{quad}, 5 = \text{pent}, 6 = \text{hex}, 7 = \text{sept}, 8 = \text{oct}, 9 = \text{enn}$, ending in -ium.
• For $Z = 114$: $1 = \text{un}, 1 = \text{un}, 4 = \text{quad} \rightarrow$ Ununquadium ($Uuq$).

Chapter 4: Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

Q13 Dipole Moment Comparison: $NH_3$ vs. $NF_3$}

The Core Basics: Dipole moment ($\mu = q \times d$) measures molecular polarity. Both $NH_3$ and $NF_3$ have trigonal pyramidal geometries with a lone pair.

Why does $NH_3$ have a higher dipole moment than $NF_3$?
• In $NH_3$, nitrogen is more electronegative than hydrogen. Thus, individual N-H bond dipoles point upward toward nitrogen, aligning in the same direction as the orbital dipole of the lone pair. They reinforce each other.
• In $NF_3$, fluorine is much more electronegative than nitrogen. The N-F bond dipoles point downward away from nitrogen, opposing the direction of the lone pair orbital dipole, which partially cancels them out.
Q14 Hybridization in Ethane & Sigma/Pi Bonds

The Core Basics: • Hybridization: Mixing of atomic orbitals of comparable energies to form new equivalent hybrid orbitals.
• In ethane ($C_2H_6$), each carbon atom is $sp^3$ hybridized, forming four directional single bonds.
• Sigma ($\sigma$) bonds form via head-on (axial) overlapping along the inter-nuclear axis.
• Pi ($\pi$) bonds form via sideways (lateral) overlapping above and below the inter-nuclear axis.

Q15 Molecular Orbital (MO) Theory: Why doesn't $He_2$ exist?

The Core Basics: MOT combines atomic orbitals to form bonding (stable) and anti-bonding (unstable) molecular orbitals. Stability is gauged by Bond Order:

Bond Order = ½ (Number of Bonding Electrons - Number of Anti-bonding Electrons)

For helium ($He_2$, 4 total electrons): $\sigma 1s^2 \sigma^* 1s^2$.
$\text{Bond Order} = \frac{2 - 2}{2} = 0$. A zero or negative bond order means the molecule cannot exist stably.

Q16 VSEPR Theory: Shape of $SF_4$}

The Core Basics: VSEPR theory states that electron pairs around a central atom repel each other. Sulfur tetrafluoride ($SF_4$) has 6 valence electrons around sulfur, forming 4 bond pairs with fluorine and possessing 1 lone pair. To minimize lone pair-bond pair repulsion, the molecule adopts a distorted see-saw geometry (trigonal bipyramidal arrangement with an equatorial lone pair).

Q17 Hydrogen Bonding Conditions & Types

The Core Basics: A hydrogen bond is an electrostatic attractive force between a hydrogen atom covalently bound to a highly electronegative atom (Fluorine, Oxygen, or Nitrogen) and another electronegative atom.
• Intermolecular H-bonding: Occurs between separate molecules (e.g., $H_2O \dots H_2O$, raising boiling points).
• Intramolecular H-bonding: Occurs within the same molecule (e.g., ortho-nitrophenol).

Chapter 5: Chemical Thermodynamics

Q18 First Law of Thermodynamics

The Core Basics: The First Law is the Law of Conservation of Energy applied to thermodynamic systems. Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed.

ΔU = Q + W

Where $\Delta U$ is change in internal energy, $Q$ is heat supplied to the system, and $W$ is work done on the system.

Q19 Under what conditions does ΔH = ΔU?

The Core Basics: Enthalpy is defined as $H = U + PV$. For a chemical reaction involving gases, $\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT$. When the change in gaseous moles ($\Delta n_g = 0$), the $RT\Delta n_g$ term becomes zero, making $\Delta H = \Delta U$.

Q20 Intensive vs. Extensive Properties

The Core Basics:
• Extensive properties depend on the total amount of matter in the system (e.g., mass, volume, enthalpy, heat capacity).
• Intensive properties are completely independent of the quantity of matter (e.g., temperature, pressure, density, refractive index). Exam Tip: If the word "molar" or "specific" precedes a property, it is always intensive.

Chapter 6: Equilibrium

Q21 Ionic Product of Water ($K_w$) & pH Calculations

The Core Basics: Water undergoes self-ionization: $H_2O \rightleftharpoons H^+ + OH^-$. The ionic product is $K_w = [H^+][OH^-] = 10^{-14} \text{ M}^2$ at $25^\circ C$.
Worked Example: For a $0.01 \text{ M}$ ($10^{-2} \text{ M}$) $OH^-$ solution, $\text{pOH} = -\log(10^{-2}) = 2$. Since $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14$, the $\text{pH} = 14 - 2 = 12$.

Q22 Conjugate Acids & Bases (Brønsted-Lowry Theory)

The Core Basics:
• Conjugate Acid: Formed by adding a proton ($H^+$) to a base (e.g., conjugate acid of $NH_3$ is $NH_4^+$).
• Conjugate Base: Formed by removing a proton ($H^-$) from an acid (e.g., conjugate base of $NH_3$ is $NH_2^-$).

Q23 Relationship Between $K_p$ and $K_c$ ($N_2 + 3H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3$)

The Core Basics: The general relation is $K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}$.
• Gaseous moles of products = 2 ($NH_3$).
• Gaseous moles of reactants = $3 + 1 = 4$ ($N_2 + 3H_2$).
• $\Delta n_g = 2 - 4 = -2$.
• Therefore, $K_p = K_c(RT)^{-2}$.

Q24 Effect of a Catalyst on Equilibrium

The Core Basics: A catalyst lowers the activation energy barrier for both forward and backward reactions equally, speeding up attainment of equilibrium. However, it cannot change the equilibrium constant ($K$) or shift equilibrium position.

Chapter 8: Organic Chemistry – Basics & Techniques

Q27 Carbocation Stability & Hyperconjugation

The Core Basics: Carbocations are positively charged carbon species. Tertiary carbocations ($(CH_3)_3C^+$) are significantly more stable than secondary and primary carbocations because they possess 9 $\alpha$-hydrogens, allowing maximum hyperconjugation (no-bond resonance) and electron-releasing inductive stabilization. Stability order: $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \text{methyl}$.

Q28 Distillation Under Reduced Pressure

The Core Basics: A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals external atmospheric pressure. Some organic compounds with extremely high boiling points decompose before reaching that temperature. By reducing surrounding pressure (using a vacuum pump), the boiling point is drastically lowered, allowing safe distillation without thermal decomposition.

Q29 Huckel's Rule of Aromaticity

The Core Basics: For a cyclic, planar, fully conjugated hydrocarbon to be aromatic, it must obey Huckel's Rule: containing exactly $(4n + 2)$ $\pi$-electrons (where $n = 0, 1, 2, 3\dots$). For benzene ($n=1$), $4(1) + 2 = 6$ $\pi$-electrons, explaining its exceptional aromatic stability.
