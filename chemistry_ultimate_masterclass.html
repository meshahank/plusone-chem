Chapter 1: Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

1. The Mole Concept & Molar Calculations

What is a Mole? In chemistry, counting atoms individually is impossible because they are infinitesimally small. A mole is simply a collection of $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ entities (atoms, molecules, or ions). This number is known as Avogadro's Number ($N_A$).

Fundamental Formulas:

Number of Moles (n) = Given Mass (W) / Molar Mass (M)
Number of Moles (n) = Given Volume at STP / 22.4 L
Number of Moles (n) = Number of Particles / 6.022 × 10²³
Worked Example: Find the number of moles and molecules in $180 \text{ g}$ of water ($H_2O$).
• Molar mass of $H_2O = (2 \times 1) + 16 = 18 \text{ g/mol}$.
• Moles ($n$) = $\frac{180}{18} = 10 \text{ moles}$.
• Molecules = $10 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 6.022 \times 10^{24}$ molecules.
2. Concentration Terms: Molarity vs. Molality

Molarity ($M$): Defined as the number of moles of solute dissolved in 1 liter of solution.

Molarity (M) = Moles of Solute (n) / Volume of Solution in Litres (V)

Molality ($m$): Defined as the number of moles of solute dissolved in 1 kilogram of solvent.

Molality (m) = Moles of Solute (n) / Mass of Solvent in Kilograms (Kg)
Crucial Examination Question: Why does Molarity change with temperature while Molality remains constant?
Explanation: Molarity involves volume ($V$), and liquids expand or contract when temperature changes. Molality involves mass ($W$), which is completely independent of temperature changes.
3. Laws of Chemical Combination

• Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier): Matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction (Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products).

• Law of Definite Proportions (Proust): A given chemical compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by mass, regardless of its source.

• Law of Multiple Proportions (Dalton): If two elements can combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in ratios of small whole numbers.

Chapter 2: Structure of Atom & Quantum Mechanics

1. Quantum Numbers & Their Significance

To completely describe an electron in an atom, four quantum numbers are required:

1. Principal Quantum Number ($n$): Denotes the main energy level (shell) and size ($n = 1, 2, 3, 4\dots$).

2. Azimuthal Quantum Number ($l$): Denotes the subshell shape ($s, p, d, f$). Values range from $0$ to $n-1$.

3. Magnetic Quantum Number ($m_l$): Denotes the spatial orientation of orbitals. Values range from $-l$ to $+l$ through $0$.

4. Spin Quantum Number ($m_s$): Denotes the direction of electron spin ($+\frac{1}{2}$ or $-\frac{1}{2}$).

2. Orbital Nodes Calculation

A node is a region where the probability of finding an electron is zero.

Angular Nodes = l
Radial Nodes = n - l - 1
Total Nodes = n - 1
Example ($3p$ orbital): Here $n = 3$, $l = 1$ (for $p$).
• Angular nodes = $1$
• Radial nodes = $3 - 1 - 1 = 1$
• Total nodes = $3 - 1 = 2$.
3. Anomalous Configurations of Chromium & Copper

• Chromium ($Z=24$): Expected $[Ar] 3d^4 4s^2$, actual is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹.

• Copper ($Z=29$): Expected $[Ar] 3d^9 4s^2$, actual is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

Why? Half-filled ($d^5$) and fully-filled ($d^{10}$) configurations possess symmetrical charge distribution and maximum exchange energy, granting extraordinary thermodynamic stability.

Chapter 3: Classification of Elements and Periodicity

1. Ionization Enthalpy & Successive Energies

Definition: The energy required to remove the outermost electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state.

Why is $IE_2$ of Sodium exceptionally high?
After losing 1 electron, $Na^+$ achieves a stable Neon noble gas octet configuration ($2,8$). Removing a second electron means breaking into this tightly-held inert core shell, requiring massive energy.
2. Isoelectronic Species & Ionic Radii

Species with the identical number of electrons are called isoelectronic (e.g., $N^{3-}, O^{2-}, F^{-}, Na^{+}, Mg^{2+}$ all have 10 electrons).

Trend: Among isoelectronic ions, anions with higher negative charge have the largest size (due to higher electron-electron repulsion), while cations with higher positive charge have the smallest size (due to stronger nuclear pull).

Chapter 4: Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

1. VSEPR Theory & Molecular Geometries

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory dictates that electron pairs around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves to minimize repulsion.

• 4 Bond Pairs, 0 Lone Pairs  ➔ Tetrahedral (109.5°) e.g., CH₄
• 3 Bond Pairs, 1 Lone Pair   ➔ Pyramidal (107°) e.g., NH₃
• 2 Bond Pairs, 2 Lone Pairs  ➔ Bent / V-Shape (104.5°) e.g., H₂O
• 4 Bond Pairs, 1 Lone Pair   ➔ See-Saw e.g., SF₄
2. Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT) & Bond Order

Atomic orbitals combine to form bonding and anti-bonding molecular orbitals.

Bond Order = ½ (Number of Bonding Electrons - Number of Anti-bonding Electrons)

• A positive bond order indicates a stable molecule exists.
• A zero or negative bond order indicates the molecule cannot exist (e.g., $He_2$).

Chapter 5: Chemical Thermodynamics

1. First Law of Thermodynamics & Enthalpy Relation

Law: Energy can neither be created nor destroyed.

ΔU = Q + W

Enthalpy Relation: $\Delta H = \Delta U + P\Delta V = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT$. When change in gaseous moles $\Delta n_g = 0$, $\Delta H = \Delta U$.

Chapter 6: Chemical & Ionic Equilibrium

1. Relation Between $K_p$ and $K_c$
Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn_g

Where $\Delta n_g = (\text{Moles of gaseous products}) - (\text{Moles of gaseous reactants})$. For $N_2 + 3H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3$, $\Delta n_g = 2 - 4 = -2$, so $K_p = K_c(RT)^{-2}$.

Chapter 7: Redox Reactions & Electrochemistry

1. Oxidation Numbers & Galvanic Cells

• In Caro's acid ($H_2SO_5$), due to peroxy linkages, sulfur exhibits an oxidation state of $+6$.

• Galvanic Cell representation: Anode (oxidation) on left, salt bridge ($||$) in center, cathode (reduction) on right: `Zn | Zn²⁺ || Cu²⁺ | Cu`.

Chapter 8: Organic Chemistry: Basics & Techniques

1. Carbocation Stability & Hyperconjugation

Tertiary carbocations $(CH_3)_3C^+$ are most stable due to 9 $\alpha$-hydrogens providing maximum hyperconjugative delocalization. Stability order: $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \text{Methyl}$.

Chapter 9: Hydrocarbons & Reaction Mechanisms

1. Markovnikov's Rule & Friedel-Crafts Reactions

• Markovnikov's Rule: In addition of $HBr$ to propene, the negative bromide ion attaches to the carbon with fewer hydrogens, yielding 2-bromopropane.

• Friedel-Crafts Acylation: Benzene reacts with acyl chloride in the presence of anhydrous $AlCl_3$ to form acetophenone.
